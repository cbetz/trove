What is a new molecular entity (NME)?

FDA's definition, how an NME differs from an NCE and from a new biologic, and how many are approved each year.

Updated November 27, 2026.

A new molecular entity (NME) is a drug whose active ingredient contains no active moiety that FDA has approved before. FDA's wording, in the Drugs@FDA glossary: an NME is "an active ingredient that contains no active moiety that has been previously approved by the Agency in an application submitted under section 505 of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, or has been previously marketed as a drug in the United States." NME is the abbreviation FDA uses for new molecular entity in its approval lists, reports and review classifications.

FDA approved 1,387 NMEs and new biologics through its Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) from 1985 through 2025, 46 of them in 2025. The year-by-year counts are on FDA drug approval statistics.

What "active moiety" means

The definition turns on the active moiety, not the product. Under 21 CFR 314.3(b), the active moiety is the molecule or ion responsible for the drug's physiological or pharmacological action, leaving out the parts that make the drug substance an ester, a salt or another noncovalent derivative such as a complex, chelate or clathrate. So a new salt or ester of an approved drug is not a new molecular entity, and neither is a new dosage form, strength or indication.

NME vs NCE: is a new molecular entity the same as a new chemical entity?

Not exactly. They are two FDA determinations made for two purposes, and FDA says so directly: its classification of a drug as an NME for review purposes "is distinct from FDA's determination of whether a drug product is a 'new chemical entity' or 'NCE' within the meaning of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act."

The regulation defines a new chemical entity as "a drug that contains no active moiety that has been approved by FDA in any other NDA submitted under section 505(b)" of the Act. For five years after such a drug is approved, FDA generally cannot accept a generic or 505(b)(2) application for a drug with the same active moiety (four years if the application challenges a patent).

Most new molecular entities are also new chemical entities, but the lists do not have to match. FDA notes that some drugs are "characterized as NMEs for administrative purposes, but nonetheless contain active moieties that are closely related to active moieties in products that FDA has previously approved."

NME vs new biologic (BLA)

Strictly, NME is a classification for drugs approved under a new drug application (NDA). Biological products such as monoclonal antibodies and enzymes are approved under a biologics license application (BLA) and have no NME classification code. FDA's novel-approval lists put the two side by side: it describes its annual list as "new molecular entities and new therapeutic biological products approved by CDER", and its compilation is the "Compilation of CDER NME and New Biologic Approvals". Industry writing often calls the biologics new biological entities (NBEs); FDA's own lists say new biologic or new therapeutic biological product.

In practice "NME" is often used loosely for the whole list. Of the 1,387 approvals in FDA's compilation for 1985–2025, 1,108 are NDAs and 279 are BLAs.

How FDA marks an NME: Type 1

On Drugs@FDA, each NDA's original approval carries a submission classification. Type 1 - New Molecular Entity is the NME code. A fixed-dose combination that contains a new molecular entity is shown as "Type 1 - New Molecular Entity and Type 4 - New Combination". Among the 130 NDAs approved from 2021 through 2024 in trove's index, Drugs@FDA showed 118 as Type 1 and 10 as Type 1 and Type 4 when the pages were read in May 2026. BLAs carry no classification code.

Each drug page on trove shows the classification FDA recorded, for example Filsuvez (Type 1) and Lybalvi (Type 1 and Type 4).

NME vs "novel drug"

FDA uses "novel" for "new drugs never before approved or marketed in the U.S." Its annual Novel Drug Approvals list is CDER's count of new molecular entities and new therapeutic biologics. It leaves out vaccines, blood products, and cell and gene therapies, which a different FDA center (CBER) approves, and it leaves out generics, biosimilars, new formulations and new indications. How many new molecular entities does FDA approve?

CDER approved an average of 46.6 novel drugs a year from 2016 through 2025, counting NMEs and new biologics together, with a low of 22 in 2016 and a high of 59 in 2018.
