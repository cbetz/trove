Hospital charity care on two public filings: what is in the difference

Nonprofit hospitals publish a financial-assistance figure on their IRS Form 990 and a charity-care figure on their Medicare cost report. This note puts the two public figures next to each other for every hospital that can be matched, with the components from each filing that show what is in the difference.

By Chris Betz · first published July 25, 2026 · revised November 19, 2026 (what changed) · public-domain data, MIT-licensed code

The two lines

The two figures, for tax year 2022:

  • IRS Form 990, Schedule H, Part I, line 7a reports financial assistance at cost: column (c) total expense, column (d) direct offsetting revenue, and column (e) net, which is (c) − (d).
  • CMS cost report (form 2552-10), Worksheet S-10, line 23, column 3 is the cost of charity care after patient payments: uninsured patients (column 1) plus insured patients (column 2).

The two lines are defined differently, so they can differ even when both are filed correctly. Three of the differences in definition are visible in the filings themselves:

  • Provider taxes. The IRS Schedule H instructions (Worksheet 1, line 4) have a filer include Medicaid or provider taxes in the line 7a cost when payments from an uncompensated care pool or DSH program in its state are intended primarily to offset the cost of financial assistance, with that pool revenue reported as offsetting revenue in column (d). When the payments are primarily to offset the cost of Medicaid services, both go to line 7b instead, and where state law does not make the purpose clear the instructions allow a proportional split. This is a classification the instructions permit, and it depends on the state.
  • S-10 line 5. The cost report puts Medicaid DSH and supplemental payments, “net of associated provider taxes or assessments”, on line 5 of Worksheet S-10. Line 5 holds the payments not already included in line 2 (Medicaid revenue); neither line is part of line 23.
  • The insured column. S-10 line 23 column 2 counts deductibles and coinsurance written off for insured patients.

The two forms also cost the care differently. Schedule H asks for the filer's most accurate costing methodology (a cost accounting system, a cost-to-charge ratio, or another method); S-10 derives cost from charges with the worksheet's own cost-to-charge ratio. And the reporting units differ: a 990 is filed by a legal entity (an EIN), a cost report by a provider (a CCN).

So a difference between the two is a reason to open both filings and read the components. It does not, by itself, say anything about either filing.

What can be set side by side

The lookup holds every EIN matched across the CMS cost report files, IRS Schedule H and a CCN-to-EIN crosswalk: 2,099 systems, of which 1,904 have both figures. The other 195 are missing one of the two lines and are shown without a difference (see missing is not zero).

The ranked index is the subset where the two lines can be set side by side without a unit or period mismatch. A row is in the index when all four hold: one CCN with a cost report under the EIN, so both filings describe one facility; the cost report's period ends within one month of the 990's; both figures are at least $1M; and total expenses on the two filings are within 5% of each other. The funnel: 2,099 matched, 1,904 with both figures, 1,797 of those period-aligned, 1,642 of those with a single cost report, and 493 in the index, in 48 states.

Among those 493, the CMS figure is the larger in 321, the IRS figure in 169, and 3 show the same amount. The median difference is 27.6% of the larger figure, and 107 differ by more than 50%. Those counts describe two differently defined lines: 143 of the 493 list offsetting revenue in column (d), and 301 show an amount on S-10 line 5. Each hospital's two figures:

Of the $77.5M difference, about $71.5M appears to be Connecticut's hospital provider tax net of the state supplemental payments it funds: the amount on S-10 line 5, which the cost report keeps separate from charity care. The worksheet behind the IRS line is not public, so this is a reconciliation from the hospital's own filings, not a figure the hospital has stated.

The return itself describes the program. Its Schedule H narrative (Part III, line 4) repeats a footnote from the system's consolidated financial statements, which says the Connecticut Disproportionate Share Hospital Program “was established to provide funds to hospitals for the provision of uncompensated care” and is funded in part by an assessment on hospital net patient service revenue. It says the system paid $317.9M into the program for the assessment in the year ended September 30, 2023, and received $198.2M in distributions. What the components show

The two filings for each of these 25 were read and sorted by what the published components show. These are reconciliations from the filings alone. They are not statements by the hospitals, and no filing says what its offsetting revenue or its line 5 consists of.

  • A · 9Column (d) or S-10 line 5 is at least half the size of the difference and points the same way: offsetting revenue in column (d) where the CMS figure is the larger (it is subtracted on the IRS line only), or a negative line 5 where the IRS figure is the larger. Yale New Haven Hospital, Tampa General Hospital, St. Peter's University Hospital, St. Joseph's Hospital (Tampa), St. Joseph's University Medical Center, Greenwich Hospital, Ascension Providence, Lawrence + Memorial Hospital, Bayfront Health St. Petersburg. For the three Connecticut hospitals it is line 5; for the other six it is column (d), which is subtracted on the IRS line and has no counterpart in S-10 line 23.
  • B · 3The IRS figure is nearer the CMS uninsured-only column (line 23 column 1) than the total; the insured column is 44%, 75% and 71% of the difference for these three. Saint Thomas West Hospital, Baptist Medical Center (Jacksonville), Saint Thomas Rutherford Hospital. Their uninsured columns are $43.6M, $39.3M and $26.0M; their insured columns $7.9M, $13.3M and $9.6M.

Missing is not zero

A line with no value on the form is shown as “not reported on this line”. It is never shown as $0, and no difference is computed for that row. 159 matched systems have no S-10 line 23 figure and 53 have a blank line 7a column (e); 195 systems are missing one or both.

Examples: The Johns Hopkins Hospital files a Worksheet S-10 with no value on line 23. St. Jude Children's Research Hospital and Texas Children's Hospital have no Worksheet S-10 in the CMS file. All three are in the lookup with their IRS figure and no difference.

Multi-hospital systems are in the lookup but not in the ranked index. Mayo Clinic's return covers 19 cost reports under one EIN. Cleveland Clinic's covers 17, one of which has no S-10 line 23, so no system total is computed for it and the partial sum is shown separately, labelled as partial.

Correction, November 19, 2026

The first version of this note, published July 25, 2026, has been revised. Three things changed.

  • The framing. The earlier version described line 7a and S-10 line 23 as directly comparable and presented the differences as two reports of the same thing that did not match. That was wrong: the two lines are defined differently, as set out above, and the difference at its lead example, Yale New Haven Hospital, is mostly the size of an amount the cost report shows on a separate line. The headline and text now say what each line measures and show the components.
  • Missing values. The earlier data showed $0 on the CMS side where the cost report had no value on line 23, or no Worksheet S-10 at all. Those rows are now shown as not reported, with no difference computed.
  • Coverage. The earlier index covered 99 hospitals. It left out the IRS's 2023 e-file release and used only the CMS fiscal year 2023 file, so a hospital whose fiscal year began before October 2022 was set against its following year's cost report and dropped from the index. With IRS releases 2023 through 2026 and the CMS fiscal year 2022 and 2023 files, the index now covers 493 hospitals. All 99 earlier rows are still in it.

Limits

  • One tax year. Everything here is tax year 2022: returns for fiscal periods ending between late 2022 and November 2023. A single year says nothing about whether a difference persists.
  • Crosswalk vintage. CCNs are linked to EINs with the Community Benefit Insight crosswalk, frozen in December 2024, which lists 2,385 EINs. 245 of the CCNs it lists for matched EINs have no cost report in the two CMS files; for 39 index rows the crosswalk lists further CCNs of that kind, and those rows are marked on the lookup page. For-profit and government hospitals file no Schedule H and are not here.
  • End-date alignment. Periods are matched on their end dates only. No index row has begin dates more than a month apart, but in the full lookup a short-period report can match on its end date; each card shows both periods.
  • Multi-CCN systems. Where one EIN covers several cost reports, the lookup shows sums across them, and no total when any one of them lacks the line. Those rows are not ranked.
  • Status. Cost reports can be as submitted, amended, settled or reopened, and a 990 can be amended. 103 index rows rest on an as-submitted cost report. Figures can change in later CMS files, and the summary record on data.cms.gov can differ slightly from the file used here.
  • The classification is by hand. The groups A to D above were assigned by reading the two filings for 25 hospitals. The other 468 index rows have not been read that way.
  • The worksheets are not public. Schedule H Worksheet 1 is not filed, so what a hospital included in line 7a can be inferred from the filings but not confirmed.
